# Team Remint — two-minute pitch for judges

The pitch presents My Climate Footprint as a teaching tool for schools. Use three voices if you have them; with two people, combine Voices 2 and 3. One person operates the game while another speaks. Read only the quoted narration.

| Time | Screen | Speaker |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00–0:22 | Team and introduction slide | Voice 1 |
| 0:22–0:45 | Game title, map and house selection | Voice 2 |
| 0:45–1:07 | Year's question | Voice 3 |
| 1:07–1:32 | House upgrade and weather | Voice 2 |
| 1:32–1:47 | Year review | Voice 3 |
| 1:47–2:00 | Team slide or team on camera | Voice 1 |

## 0:00–0:22 — Make it personal

**Screen:** Begin with the team on camera, then show `theslide.pdf` as the opening continues.

**Voice 1:** “Hi, we're Team Remint. Think of a young person you care about. One day, they may buy a home or lead a community. They'll make those decisions in a changing climate. They deserve to understand the risks and know what they can do.”

## 0:22–0:45 — Introduce the classroom tool

**Screen:** Show the game's title, then press Start. Briefly show the regions on the map. Choose Urban, then buy the 1950s weatherboard bungalow in River Valley.

**Voice 2:** “We built My Climate Footprint, a game teachers can use to make climate change personal. Students choose a home and try to protect it for ten years. Here by the river, we'll prepare for floods. In the hills, we'd face landslides.”

## 0:45–1:07 — Give the class a decision

**Screen:** The year's question opens after the house is bought. Hold briefly on the choices, select an answer, show its feedback, then close the question.

**Voice 3:** “Each year brings a choice about transport, waste or buildings. Teachers can ask students to vote and explain why. The game imagines everyone choosing the same way. It shows how shared decisions can increase or reduce the pollution warming our planet, changing disaster risk in the game.”

## 1:07–1:32 — Put the choice into action

**Screen:** Open a + marker and buy **Seal doors**. Show the upgraded house, then press **Finish Upgrades**. Let the calendar and weather animation play.

**Voice 2:** “Now we have limited money to protect our house. We'll seal the doors against flooding. Students have to think: what matters most, and what can wait? Then the year passes, and they see what happens to the home they've invested in.”

## 1:32–1:47 — Turn the result into a lesson

**Screen:** Press **See the year review**. Hold on the **Cause**, **Effect** and **What helped** panels.

**Voice 3:** “The review explains what happened and what could help. A teacher can ask: what would you change next time? Students can try again, learning from mistakes before the stakes are real.”

## 1:47–2:00 — Close

**Screen:** Return to the team slide or show all team members on camera.

**Voice 1:** “Our COP31 goal is climate resilient cities. Tomorrow's homeowners and leaders are in classrooms today. Let's help them protect the places they'll call home. Thank you.”

## Recording notes

- The narration is about 230 words. The timings are rehearsal targets; practise with the clicks to fit two minutes.
- Pause briefly after “a young person you care about” and “before the stakes are real.” Let the final line land before saying “Thank you.”
- The first-time guide appears after Start. Dismiss it while recording, then cut that moment from the final video.
- The year's question changes between games. Choose an answer that fits the narration; there is no need to name a particular question.
- The weather result is random. The review works whether a disaster hits or not. If a disaster hits, pause briefly on the damaged house.
- The game's prices and disaster chances are teaching values, not real forecasts for an individual home. Avoid presenting them as predictions.

## If judges ask

**Who is it for?** Teachers and schools can use it with students who may become homeowners. The game gives a class concrete choices to discuss before those students make housing decisions for themselves.

**How could a teacher use it?** Project the game, let the class choose a home and vote on decisions, then use the year review to discuss what helped and what they would change. This is a proposed classroom use of the current game.

**Are the disaster chances real forecasts?** No. They are game values used to show how location, preparation and shared choices interact. Hazard maps informed which areas face floods and which face landslides.

**Why does one player's choice affect the disaster chance?** The game asks players to imagine everyone making the same choice. The meter represents that shared effect, not one household causing a flood or landslide.
